The Case Study

Five-Step Walkthrough

1
Why Turnover Data Needs the Same Audit Lens
Quits, hires, and layoffs rates are a confidence signal, not just a headcount tally
▾

A billing audit asks whether what's charged matches what actually happened. Labor market turnover data asks a structurally similar question at the macro scale: do employers and workers actually behave the way headline "jobs added" numbers suggest, once you look at quits, hires, and layoffs as a system instead of one number at a time?

The quits rate is the single most useful line in the whole JOLTS release — people only quit voluntarily when they're confident about their next move, so it functions as a real-time confidence survey with a paycheck attached. Pair it with layoffs and job openings, and the three series together describe whether a labor market is tight, cooling, or already slack — well before that shows up in the unemployment rate headline.

  • Quits rate — worker-side confidence: falling quits means workers are hunkering down, not finding better options
  • Layoffs rate — employer-side stress: a lagging indicator that usually confirms what quits already signaled
  • Job openings vs. unemployment (V/U ratio) — the classic Beveridge curve framing of how tight the market actually is
2
The Dataset & Sourcing
National JOLTS rates and the Beveridge curve inputs, Jan 2022 – Sep/Apr 2026
▾

Two real BLS series feed this analysis, both seasonally adjusted, national, total nonfarm:

JOLTS Rates
Hires · Quits · Layoffs
52 months, Jan 2022 – Apr 2026. Series IDs JTS000000000000000HIR / QUR / LDR — verified against data.bls.gov/timeseries directly.
Beveridge Inputs
Job Openings · Unemployment Level
45 months, Jan 2022 – Sep 2025. Openings from JOLTS (JTS...JOL); unemployment level from the CPS household survey (LNS13000000), not JOLTS itself.

Both feed labor_market_analysis.R, which merges them into one tidy monthly table with a derived V/U ratio column — the same shape either loads cleanly into R for modeling or into Excel for the dashboard layer.

3
Statistical Methods — the R Layer
STL decomposition, a Beveridge curve regression, and a leading-indicator test
▾

Three real statistical methods, each answering a different question the z-score anomaly detectors in the rest of this portfolio don't touch:

STL Decomposition
stl(quits_ts, s.window="periodic")
Splits the quits rate into trend, seasonal, and remainder components — isolating the real multi-year cooldown from routine monthly noise.
Beveridge Curve Regression
lm(openings_k ~ unemployment_k)
Slope = −2.58, R² = 0.65, p < 0.001. A strong, statistically significant negative relationship — textbook Beveridge curve behavior confirmed on real 2022–2025 data.
Quits → Layoffs (3-Month Lead)
lm(layoffs_lead3 ~ quits_rate)
Slope = −0.11, R² = 0.185, p = 0.002. Statistically significant: a cooling quits rate does precede rising layoffs — but explains under a fifth of the variance, so it's a signal to watch, not a forecast to trust alone.
R
# Beveridge curve: openings regressed on unemployment
